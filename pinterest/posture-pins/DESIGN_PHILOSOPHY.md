# Plumb Line

**A visual philosophy of quiet measurement.**

Plumb Line treats the body as something to observe closely rather than decorate. Every composition starts from one vertical, a single hairline dropped through space the way a mason checks a wall. Forms gather around that line: some lean away from it, some settle onto it. Tension and relief are communicated through deviation and return rather than through explanation. The work should feel like a plate from a patient natural-history atlas. It is calm and exact, and made by a hand with the steadiness to draw that line without a tremor.

Space is the primary material. Figures are solid, unbroken silhouettes in one deep ink, cut with the precision of a paper artist. Limbs taper like real anatomy, and joints resolve in soft curves. There are no faces or ornament; posture alone says everything. Everything the figure touches is drawn in a contrasting register: fine architectural linework for chairs, desks and screens, as if taken from a furniture maker's elevation drawing. The weight of the ink body against the lightness of the line world is the central visual chord. It must be balanced with painstaking care until neither side overpowers the other.

Color is disciplined and warm. The ground is unbleached paper with a faint grain you feel before you see. The ink is a blue-black. A single fired terracotta marks what matters most: the line of alignment, a point of strain, a direction of movement. A muted sage may appear rarely as the quiet signal of balance restored. Nothing else earns color. Each hue should look calibrated over many sittings, the product of deep expertise rather than a default swatch.

Typography behaves like the captions of a scientific plate. It stays sparse and exact, and it is always subordinate to the drawing. A refined high-contrast serif carries the one large statement, sometimes turning to italic for a single word of emphasis. A restrained sans handles short labels. A monospaced face supplies reference marks such as figure numbers, angles and measurements, set small, widely tracked and quietly clinical. Text never explains in paragraphs. It points, names and measures, then gets out of the way. Every baseline, margin and leader line is placed with master-level precision, and nothing touches or crowds anything else.

Rhythm comes from systematic repetition. Plates share one grid, one margin and one set of annotation conventions: hairline leaders ending in small open circles, dimension lines with ticked ends, angle arcs with their degree value. A series of plates should read like chapters of the same meticulously crafted atlas. Each one is distinct, yet all of them are unmistakably drawn by the same exacting hand after countless hours of refinement.
